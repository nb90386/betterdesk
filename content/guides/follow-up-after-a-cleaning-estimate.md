# A simple follow-up process after a cleaning estimate

You walked the building, wrote a careful proposal and sent it. Then nothing. Most owners either never follow up, or follow up so often it feels like chasing.

There's a middle path. A short, planned sequence that is useful to the buyer, easy to say no to, and ends on a clear date. This guide lays one out.

## Why follow up at all

Facility managers are busy. Your proposal may be sitting under three others, waiting on a budget meeting, or forwarded to someone you've never met. Silence usually means "not yet," not "no."

A good follow-up makes the decision easier. It answers a question, clarifies scope, or confirms timing. A bad one just asks "any update?"

## Before you send the proposal

Settle two things during the walkthrough or the call when you send the proposal:

- **When they expect to decide.** Ask directly: "When are you planning to choose?"
- **How they want to hear from you.** Email, phone, text? Who else should be copied?

Then put the follow-up dates on your calendar before you hit send. If you don't schedule it, it won't happen.

## A follow-up sequence that respects their time

Adjust the timing to their decision date. If they said "end of the month," don't push before then. This is a starting point for a typical bid.

### Day 0: Send the proposal

Send it the way they asked. In the email:

- One line thanking them for the walkthrough
- One line repeating their main concern in their words
- The proposal attached (PDF), with a short summary in the email body
- A clear next step: "Happy to walk through any of this on a 10-minute call."

### Day 2 or 3: Confirm it arrived

Keep it short. Proposals get caught in spam filters or forwarded and lost.

For example: *"Hi Dana, just making sure the proposal for the Colfax office came through. If anything in the scope doesn't match what you need, tell me and I'll adjust it."*

### Around day 7: Add something useful

Don't just ask for an update. Give them something:

- An answer to a question they raised on the walkthrough
- A clarification, like how you'd handle the Friday restroom issue they mentioned
- An alternative, such as a lower-frequency option if budget seemed tight
- A reference they can call

### Around day 14: Ask a direct question

By now, ask plainly where things stand. Make it easy to answer, including "no."

For example: *"Are you still planning to decide this month? If you've gone another way, that's fine. A quick reply helps me plan crew schedules."*

### Around day 21 to 30: Close the loop

Send one last message and say it's the last.

For example: *"I haven't heard back, so I'll assume the timing isn't right and stop following up. If anything changes, I'm a call away. Thanks again for showing me the building."*

Then stop. This message often gets a reply, because it's honest and asks nothing.

## What to say, and what not to say

### Do

- Use their name and their building's name
- Refer to something specific from the walkthrough
- Keep each message to a few sentences
- Make one clear ask per message
- Make it easy to say no

### Don't

- Invent deadlines ("this price is only good until Friday") unless it's true
- Criticize their current cleaner
- Call and email on the same day
- Send "just checking in" with nothing new
- Keep going after they've said no, or after your final message

Pressure tactics might win a contract occasionally. They also make facility managers wary of you for the next bid, and they talk to each other.

## When to stop

Stop when any of these happen:

- They say no, or that they chose someone else
- They ask you to stop
- You've sent your final close-the-loop message

If they chose someone else, a short reply is worth sending: "Thanks for letting me know. If anything changes, I'd be glad to bid again." Then add a reminder to check in near their contract renewal, if they told you when that is. Ask permission first: "Mind if I reach out before your renewal next year?"

## CAN-SPAM basics for business email

The US CAN-SPAM Act applies to commercial email, and the Federal Trade Commission's [compliance guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business) is clear on one point many owners miss: "The law makes no exception for business-to-business email."

This is a plain summary, not legal advice.

### Does it apply to your follow-ups?

CAN-SPAM covers any message whose "primary purpose" is "the commercial advertisement or promotion" of a product or service. The FTC says "It covers all commercial messages," not only bulk email. Some messages count as "transactional or relationship" instead, such as one that "Facilitates, completes, or confirms a commercial transaction that the recipient already has agreed to." The FTC adds that "the law views these categories narrowly."

A follow-up on a proposal the buyer hasn't accepted yet is a promotion of your services. The safe approach is to treat follow-ups and any marketing emails as commercial and meet the requirements below.

### The main requirements

From the FTC guide:

1. "Don't use false or misleading header information." Your From name and address should accurately identify you.
2. "Don't use deceptive subject lines." "Proposal for Colfax office cleaning" is fine. "Re: your invoice" when there's no invoice is not.
3. "Identify the message as an ad." The FTC says "The law gives you a lot of leeway in how to do this," but the disclosure must be clear.
4. "Tell recipients where you're located." Include a valid physical postal address. The FTC says this can be your street address, "a post office box you've registered with the U.S. Postal Service," or a registered private mailbox.
5. "Tell recipients how to opt out of receiving future marketing email from you." A line like "Reply 'stop' and I won't email you again" works if you honor it.
6. "Honor opt-out requests promptly." The FTC says "within 10 business days," and you "can't sell or transfer their email addresses."
7. "Monitor what others are doing on your behalf." If someone sends email for you, you are still responsible.

The FTC says each email that breaks the law can bring penalties "of up to $53,088." For a small company, that makes it worth adding an address and opt-out line to your email signature now.

### A signature that covers address and opt-out

- Jordan Reyes, Owner
- Clearline Commercial Cleaning
- 1234 Example St, Denver, CO 80220
- (303) 555-0142
- Don't want follow-ups? Reply "stop" and I'll take you off my list.

The name, address and phone above are fictional placeholders. A signature handles the address and opt-out rules. It doesn't handle the ad-disclosure rule; if you're unsure how that applies to your proposal emails, ask a lawyer.

## Worked example: Clearline Commercial Cleaning (fictional)

*Clearline Commercial Cleaning is a made-up company used to illustrate this process.*

Clearline's owner sends a proposal to the office manager of a two-floor dental office on a Tuesday. On the walkthrough, she said the practice owner decides at the end-of-month partner meeting.

- **Tuesday:** Proposal sent with a three-line summary.
- **Thursday:** "Did it arrive?" note. She replies that it did and asks if Saturday cleaning is possible.
- **The next Tuesday:** The owner answers the Saturday question with a price for that option and a reference from another clinic.
- **After the partner meeting:** A direct question: "Did the partners get a chance to review it?" She says they want one change to the restroom schedule.
- The owner sends a revised proposal the same day. The practice signs two days later.

If she had gone quiet, the owner had a final message ready for day 30, and would have stopped there.
