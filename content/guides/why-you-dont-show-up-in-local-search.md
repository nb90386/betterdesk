# Why your cleaning company doesn't show up for local searches

You search "office cleaning near me" from your own truck and see three competitors and not you. It's frustrating, and it usually has a fixable cause.

This guide walks through the common ones in order: your Google Business Profile, your website's service and area pages, consistent business details, reviews, and technical problems that stop Google reading your site. It ends with what nobody can control.

Everything here is based on Google's own published guidance, linked inline.

## How Google decides local results

Google's [local ranking help page](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091) names three factors:

- **Relevance:** "how well a Business Profile matches what someone is searching for."
- **Distance:** "how far each business is from the customer who's searching."
- **Prominence:** "how well-known a business is."

It also says: "There's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google." Anyone who promises a guaranteed spot in the map results is promising something Google says can't be bought.

You can't change distance. You can improve relevance and, over time, prominence.

## 1. Your Google Business Profile

For most local searches, the Business Profile is what appears first. If it's missing, unverified or half filled in, that's the first thing to fix.

### Verify it

Google says verification "tells Google that you're authorized to represent the business" ([local ranking tips](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091)). An unverified profile may not show at all.

### Set it up as a service-area business

Most commercial cleaners go to the customer. Customers don't come to you. Google's [guidelines for representing your business](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177) say:

- "If you're a service-area business, you should hide your business address from customers."
- You "should have one profile for the central office or location with a designated service area."
- Your service area "shouldn't extend farther than about 2 hours of driving time."

Set a service area you actually cover. Listing every county in the state doesn't make you more relevant, and it breaks Google's guidance.

### Use your real business name

The same guidelines say "Including unnecessary information in your business name isn't permitted." Your name field should match your signage, invoices and registration. "Clearline Commercial Cleaning" is fine. "Clearline Commercial Cleaning | Best Office Cleaners Denver" is not.

### Fill in everything else

Google says to "Make sure your business info is as complete as possible." That means:

- Primary category, plus secondary categories that match real services
- Phone number and website
- Hours, including when people can reach you
- A short description of what you do and who you serve
- Services listed individually
- Real photos of your crew, vans and work (not stock images)

## 2. Your website's service and area pages

Your website feeds relevance. If your site has one "Services" page that lists ten things in a sentence, Google has little to match against a search for "medical office cleaning."

### One page per main service

Give each main service its own page: office cleaning, medical office cleaning, floor care, post-construction cleanup, day porter, whatever you actually sell. Each page should say plainly:

- What the service includes
- Who it's for
- How often you typically do it
- Which areas you serve
- How to request a quote

### Area pages, done carefully

It's reasonable to have a page for each real area you serve, if each page has something genuinely specific to say: buildings you clean there, how you schedule crews in that area, local access quirks.

What you should not do is generate dozens of near-identical city pages. Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) list as doorway abuse "Having multiple domain names or pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page." They also list "Blocks of text that list cities and regions that a web page is trying to rank for" as keyword stuffing.

A short, honest list of areas you serve is fine. A footer with 80 suburb names is not.

## 3. Consistent name, address and phone

Use one business name, one phone number and one address (or service area) everywhere: your website footer, your Business Profile, Bing Places, Apple Business Connect, Yelp, your local chamber and any trade directories.

Old phone numbers and old addresses linger online for years. If Google sees two versions of your business with different details, it's harder to be confident they're the same company. Search your company name and your old phone number to find stragglers, then update or remove them.

## 4. Reviews you're allowed to ask for

Reviews are part of prominence, and buyers read them. Google is clear about how you may get them.

### What's allowed

Google's [review tips](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122) say "you can ask customers to visit a Google link or scan a QR code." Create your review link in your Business Profile and send it to clients after good work.

Reply to reviews. Google says replying "shows that you value their feedback" ([local ranking tips](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091)). Keep replies short and specific. Answer negative ones calmly and offer to fix the problem offline.

### What isn't allowed

Google's [Maps content policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) lists these as prohibited for businesses:

- To "Offer incentives – such as payment, discounts, free goods and/or services" for reviews
- To "Discourage or prohibit negative reviews, or selectively solicit positive reviews from customers"
- "Content that is based on a conflict of interest," which can include current or former employment

In plain terms: no gift cards for reviews, no asking only the happy clients, and no reviews from your staff or family. Ask every client the same way.

## 5. Crawl and index problems on your website

Sometimes the problem is that Google can't read your site at all. These are quick to check.

### Is your site in Google?

Search `site:yourdomain.com` in Google. If nothing comes up, or only your home page does, Google either hasn't found your pages or isn't indexing them.

Set up [Google Search Console](https://search.google.com/search-console) for your site. Its URL Inspection tool tells you whether a specific page is indexed and, if not, why.

### Check robots.txt

Open `yourdomain.com/robots.txt`. If you see `Disallow: /` under `User-agent: *` or `User-agent: Googlebot`, you're blocking Google from crawling your whole site. This sometimes gets left behind when a site moves from staging to live.

Google's [robots.txt guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/robots/intro) explains that robots.txt "tells search engine crawlers which URLs the crawler can access on your site." It also notes it "is not a mechanism for keeping a web page out of Google." That job belongs to a `noindex` tag.

### Check for a stray noindex

Some website builders have a "discourage search engines" or "hide from search" setting. If it's on, your pages carry a `noindex` tag and won't appear. URL Inspection in Search Console will show this.

### Structured data that matches the page

Structured data (code that labels your name, phone and hours) is optional. If you use it, Google's [structured data guidelines](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/sd-policies) say it "must be a true representation of the page content." Don't mark up services, areas or hours the page doesn't show. And don't add star ratings for your own business. Google's [review snippet rules](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/review-snippet) make those pages "ineligible for star review feature."

## What you can't control

Be realistic about the limits:

- **Distance.** A searcher two miles from a competitor's office and twenty from yours will often see them first.
- **Who searches, and from where.** Results differ by location, device and wording.
- **Competitors' history.** A company with ten years of reviews and links has a head start.
- **Google's changes.** Google updates how results work, and doesn't publish the details.

Focus on what you can fix, check your progress in Search Console and your Business Profile performance, and give changes a few weeks to show.

## A quick self-check

1. Search your exact business name. Does your profile appear, with the right phone and hours?
2. Is your profile verified, with the address hidden and a realistic service area?
3. Does your business name field match your real name, with no extra keywords?
4. Does each main service have its own page on your site?
5. Are the areas you list real places you send crews?
6. Do your name, phone and address match across your site, Google, Bing and directories?
7. When did you last ask a client for a review, and have you replied to recent ones?
8. Does `site:yourdomain.com` show your service pages?
9. Is robots.txt free of a site-wide `Disallow: /`?

## Worked example: Clearline Commercial Cleaning (fictional)

*Clearline Commercial Cleaning is a made-up company used to illustrate this guide. Nothing here describes real search results.*

Clearline's owner notices they never show up for "commercial cleaning central Denver." They work through the list:

- The Business Profile shows the owner's home address in Aurora. They hide it and set a service area covering central and east Denver.
- The name field reads "Clearline Commercial Cleaning Denver Office Janitorial." They change it to "Clearline Commercial Cleaning."
- The website has one Services page. They write separate pages for office cleaning, medical office cleaning and floor care.
- The site footer lists 40 Colorado towns. They replace it with the six areas they actually cover.
- Their old phone number is still on two directories. They update both.
- They create a review link and add it to the end-of-month check-in email that goes to every client.
- `site:` search shows only the home page. Search Console reveals the builder's "hide from search engines" box was still ticked. They untick it and request indexing.

None of this guarantees a top spot. It removes the reasons Google had for leaving them out.
