# How to prepare for a commercial cleaning walkthrough

The walkthrough is where most cleaning bids are won or lost. You get maybe 30 to 60 minutes in the building. Whatever you miss turns into guesswork in the price, and guesswork turns into either a lost bid or a losing account.

This guide covers what to do before you arrive, what to measure and count on site, what to ask the facility contact, and how to turn your notes into a proposal the same week.

## Before the walkthrough

### Confirm the basics by phone or email

Ask these before you drive over:

- **Who will meet you,** and are they the person who decides? If not, who is?
- **What type of facility** is it: office, clinic, school, gym, warehouse office, retail?
- **Roughly how big** is the space, and how many floors?
- **Why are they looking?** New space, unhappy with the current cleaner, or a contract ending?
- **When do they want to start?**
- **Can you see every area,** including restrooms, break rooms and any locked rooms?

If the answer to the last one is no, ask for a second visit or a floor plan. Pricing rooms you never saw is how scope disputes start.

### Look them up

Spend ten minutes on their website and the building's listing. Note the business type, the hours, and anything that hints at special needs: patient areas, food prep, labs, high foot traffic, or after-hours security.

### Bring the right kit

- A laser distance measure (or a long tape)
- A printed floor plan if they can send one
- Your walkthrough checklist (paper or phone)
- A phone camera, with permission to take photos
- A business card and a one-page summary of your company
- Your certificate of insurance, in case they ask

## During the walkthrough

Walk every area in a set order, for example front door, offices, conference rooms, break room, restrooms, back of house. Use the same order every time so you don't skip anything.

### What to measure

Get real cleanable square footage by area type. Don't rely on the lease number. Leases often quote rentable area, which can include shared space you won't be cleaning.

Record square footage separately for:

- Private offices and open office areas
- Conference and training rooms
- Restrooms
- Break rooms and kitchens
- Lobbies and corridors
- Stairwells and elevators, if in scope
- Storage, warehouse or mechanical rooms, if in scope

Area types matter because they take very different amounts of time per square foot. A restroom and an open office of the same size are not the same job.

### What to count

Square footage alone won't price a restroom or a break room. Count the things that take time:

- Toilets, urinals and sinks
- Showers, if any
- Paper towel, soap and toilet paper dispensers
- Trash and recycling bins, and where the outside dumpster is
- Break-room appliances you would clean: microwaves, fridges, coffee machines
- Interior glass doors and partitions, and entrance glass
- Desks, if they expect desks wiped (many offices only want clear desks wiped)

### Floor types

Note each floor type and its condition. Carpet, vinyl tile, luxury vinyl plank, ceramic tile, sealed concrete and hardwood all need different equipment and different periodic work.

For hard floors, ask when they were last stripped, refinished or deep cleaned. For carpet, ask when it was last extracted. Periodic work is usually priced separately from nightly cleaning, so note it as its own line.

### Access, security and logistics

These details rarely appear in the request but cost you time and trust if you miss them:

- Building hours and the cleaning window
- Keys, fobs, alarm codes, and who is on the alarm call list
- Where crews can park and which door they use
- The janitor closet: is there one, does it lock, is there a mop sink and water?
- Who supplies consumables: paper, soap, liners
- Any rooms that are off limits or need an escort

### Safety and special requirements

Ask what chemicals are already stored on site and whether they have rules about products you bring. OSHA's Hazard Communication standard says "All employers with hazardous chemicals in their workplaces must have labels and safety data sheets for their exposed workers" ([OSHA Hazard Communication](https://www.osha.gov/hazcom)). Make sure your own crew's products and data sheets are in order before you start.

For medical, dental and veterinary sites, ask directly about sharps, regulated waste and exam-room cleaning expectations. OSHA notes that "housekeeping personnel in some industries" may be at risk for exposure to bloodborne pathogens ([OSHA Bloodborne Pathogens](https://www.osha.gov/bloodborne-pathogens)). Be clear in the proposal about what your crew will and will not handle, and check your own obligations before taking the work.

### Photos

Ask before taking photos. Take wide shots of each area and close-ups of problem spots: stained carpet, buildup in grout, damaged fixtures. These help you price accurately and protect you later if someone asks whether a stain was there before you started.

## Questions to ask the facility contact

Good questions show you've done this before. They also surface the real reasons someone will choose you.

### About the current situation

- What does your current cleaner do well?
- What's the one thing that made you start looking?
- How do complaints reach the cleaner today, and how fast are they fixed?
- What are people in the building complaining about most?

### About scope and frequency

- Which areas need nightly cleaning, and which need it weekly?
- Are there days the building is closed or unusually busy?
- Do you need day porter coverage, or after-hours only?
- Is periodic floor work or window cleaning part of this bid, or separate?

### About the decision

- Who else is involved in choosing?
- How many companies are you asking to bid?
- Is there a budget range you're working within?
- What contract length and notice period do you expect?
- What insurance limits and documents will you need from us?
- When will you decide, and how should we send the proposal?

### About what good looks like

- If this goes well, what will be different in three months?
- How would you like us to report on our work: a checklist, a monthly visit, a quick text?

Write the answers down in their words. Reusing their phrasing in the proposal shows you listened.

## After the walkthrough

### Write it up the same day

Your memory fades fast after a few walkthroughs in a row. Within a few hours, turn your notes into a clean summary: areas, square footage by type, counts, floor types, access notes, special requirements, and their answers to your questions.

### Turn measurements into hours

Use your own production rates. These are the square feet per hour your crews actually clean for each area type, based on your past jobs. Published industry rates exist, but your crews, equipment and standards are what matter. If you don't track this yet, start timing a few jobs.

For each area: square footage divided by your rate, times how many times per week. Add fixed time for restrooms and break rooms based on your counts. Add travel and setup time. That gives you weekly labor hours.

### Build the proposal

A clear proposal usually has:

1. **A short summary** of what they told you they need, in their words.
2. **A scope table** listing each area and what will be done there.
3. **A frequency schedule**: nightly, weekly, monthly and periodic tasks.
4. **What's included and what isn't**, such as consumables, periodic floor work, window cleaning and supply restocking.
5. **How you'll communicate and handle issues.**
6. **Price**, with periodic work priced on its own line.
7. **Start date, contract term and notice period.**
8. **Your assumptions.** For example: "Pricing assumes after-hours access from 6 p.m. and a lockable janitor closet with a mop sink."

Assumptions protect both sides. If something changes, you have a clear basis for revisiting the price.

### Set the follow-up date before you send it

Put the follow-up on your calendar the moment the proposal goes out. Our guide to [following up after a cleaning estimate](/resources/guides/follow-up-after-a-cleaning-estimate) covers what to say and when to stop.

## Worked example: Clearline Commercial Cleaning (fictional)

*Clearline Commercial Cleaning is a made-up company used to illustrate this checklist. The facility below is also invented.*

Clearline's owner walks a two-floor dental and administrative office in east Denver. The contact is the office manager. The practice owner makes the final call.

On site, the owner records about 6,200 cleanable square feet across offices, four operatories, a lab, two restrooms with six fixtures in total, a break room and a lobby. Floors are luxury vinyl plank in patient areas and carpet in offices. The office manager says the current cleaner misses restrooms on Fridays and nobody returns their calls.

The owner asks about sharps and regulated waste. The practice has a medical waste vendor, and the crew will not touch sharps containers. That goes in the proposal as an exclusion.

That evening, the owner writes up the notes and calculates weekly hours from Clearline's own rates. The proposal opens by repeating the office manager's two concerns: Friday restrooms and response time. It lists a named contact and a same-day response commitment for issues reported before 3 p.m. It prices carpet extraction twice a year on its own line, and states the assumption of access after 6 p.m. through the rear door.

The follow-up is on the calendar for two business days later.
